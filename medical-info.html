What is cervical cancer?

The cervix is the narrow lower part of the womb, at the top of the vagina. Cervical cancer starts when cells in the cervix change and begin to grow out of control. Almost every case is caused by a persistent infection with the human papillomavirus (HPV).

It is one of the most preventable cancers there is. Changes in the cervix usually take many years to become cancer, which gives us time to find and treat them — if women are able to be screened.

HPV and how it spreads

HPV is a very common family of viruses. Most people who are sexually active will have HPV at some point, and in most cases the body clears it on its own without any symptoms. Cancer develops when the infection persists for years and slowly changes the cells of the cervix.

  • HPV spreads through skin-to-skin genital contact, so it can be passed on even without full intercourse.
  • A person can carry HPV for years without knowing, and can pass it on to someone who will show no symptoms either.
  • Condoms do not prevent HPV completely. They lower the risk, but they are not full protection.
  • Having HPV is not a sign of promiscuity. It is very common, and it is manageable.

HPV is not a verdict.

A positive HPV result does not mean you have cancer. It means your body needs a closer look, which we can arrange — and where treatment is needed, it can often be done the same day.

Vaccination

The HPV vaccine protects against the virus that causes most cervical cancers. It is safe, and it is the single best way to cut a girl’s lifetime risk.

Most effective at 9–14

The vaccine works best when it is given early, before any exposure to the virus. That is why girls aged 9 to 14 are the priority, and why we encourage parents and guardians to bring their daughters.

All women are encouraged

Protection is strongest when the vaccine comes first, but it still has value later. All women of reproductive age are encouraged to be vaccinated. Ask our team what it can offer you.

Vaccination and screening work together. Even if you have been vaccinated, screening remains important, so keep your screening appointments.

Screening

Screening looks for HPV, or for early cell changes, before any symptoms appear. It is quick and is the way most cervical cancers are stopped before they start. We focus on women aged 20 and above, with no upper age limit.

Our test

Rapid HPV E6/E7 test. Our mobile clinic uses a rapid point-of-care HPV E6/E7 Oncoprotein test, so there is no long wait for a laboratory and you can have your result the same day.

Closer look

Colposcopy. If a result needs a closer look, a clinician examines the cervix with a magnifying instrument.

Other tests

Pap smear. A laboratory test that looks for abnormal cells. It needs a sample to be sent away and read.

Screening is for women who feel perfectly well.

Early cervical changes almost never cause symptoms. By the time something feels wrong, it is usually later than we would like. Don’t wait to feel unwell.

Looking after yourself

Get screened

Come for an HPV screen even if you feel well and even if you were screened before. Ask our team how often you should come back; your clinician will advise you.

Vaccination

All women of reproductive age are encouraged to be vaccinated against HPV. Ask about it at your visit.

Condoms

Condoms lower your chance of HPV and protect against other infections too, but they do not prevent HPV completely, which is why screening matters.

Your status

If you are living with HIV, tell our clinician if you are comfortable doing so. Persistent HPV is more likely, so regular screening matters even more. This stays between you and the clinical team.

A positive result

It does not mean cancer. Most positive results lead to a closer look and, where needed, treatment of early changes — often the same day.

For peer educators and outreach workers

If you support other women, you are often the person they trust most. You can help by sharing the message that screening is free and confidential, that treatment of early changes is simple, and that nobody will be asked about their work. Your privacy comes first.

Your results are shared only with the people involved in your care. Not with an employer, a landlord, family, a partner or anyone else, unless you ask us to.

Signs and symptoms

Early cervical cancer often has no symptoms at all, which is why screening matters. As it grows, some women notice:

  • Unusual bleeding — between periods, after sex, or after menopause.
  • Unusual discharge — watery, bloody or foul-smelling, especially if it doesn’t go away.
  • Pelvic pain or pain during sex that doesn’t settle.

These signs can have other causes, but any of them is a good reason to be checked by a health worker soon.

Treatment

Early changes (precancer)

When screening finds early changes, treatment is simple, protects the cervix, and can often be done in a single visit:

  • Cryotherapy — freezing the abnormal cells with a cold probe.
  • Thermal ablation — using heat to destroy the abnormal cells.
  • LEEP — removing the affected tissue with a thin wire loop under local anaesthetic.

Where treatment is needed, we aim to provide it on the same day. Where more investigation is needed, we link you to it.

Cervical cancer

If cancer has developed, treatment depends on how far it has spread and may include surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or a combination. Palliative care supports comfort and quality of life. Outcomes are far better when it is found early.
